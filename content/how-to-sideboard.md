## How to sideboard with this deck

The first question in every matchup is which turn you want to go off. Everything else is massaging the numbers for that turn, choosing the pivot, and answering the permanents that stop it. If the answer is turn two to four, you are jamming. If it is turn five or later, trim Dreadnoughts and enablers so the first one you cast goes the distance. If the answer is never, cut them and Freeze.

**What usually leaves, and why.** None of this is a rule; every row in the matchup chapters is the judgment applied, and the judgment is what follows.

- Thwart leaves when it can't do its job: when you won't have three Islands on the table on the turn you jam, so it can't protect the Dreadnought; when giving back three land drops is too big a commitment in that matchup; or when the matchup simply doesn't need that much protection. It also competes with Gush for the same Islands, so in the matchups where Gush is the engine, Thwart is at its worst. It stays where it is the free counter you need after your hand has been taken apart.

- Brain Freeze leaves from every plan that isn't a Freeze plan, where it is a dead card.

- Flash of Insight leaves when you are jamming, because it is slow, and stays in every long game.

- Peek is the first cantrip to go, because it selects nothing. Its value is information, and post-board that information is worth less: expect every opponent to have brought at least one answer to the Dreadnought, so the coast is rarely as clear as Peek says. It stays where knowing the coast is clear lets you go a turn earlier, as against Elves.

- Opt goes only when something has to and you would rather keep Peek. Impulse doesn't go.

- Daze is a read on the opponent, not a rule: it stays on the play against opponents who tap out, and leaves against opponents who have shown they will play around it, or on the draw against decks that don't tap out.

- Foil is trimmed where the pitch cost spends the resource they are attacking — Islands against land destruction, cards against heavy discard, where a hand under Duress and Therapy can't afford to pay two for a counter — and where the game is attrition with no commit turn to overload.

- Keg leaves when its targets do, when no one setting catches enough of their board, or when Chain does its job better, as in the mirror. Chain mostly doesn't: I keep one copy in the deck in nearly every matchup as insurance against the thing that got through permission — a lock piece, a threat too big to race, something that needs countering on the way down a second time or bouncing for a turn of tempo. One copy in a deck that cantrips this heavily is an out you will find; without it, the same board is a scoop.

- Dreadnoughts and Stifles move only with the plan: cut one to three Dreadnoughts and one or two Stifles on build-up or Freeze plans, with Stifle scaling down with the Dreadnought count except where it has a job of its own; against Replenish, all of them go, and against Enchantress all the Dreadnoughts go while the Stifles stay for the Gaea's Blessing trigger.

**What comes in, and for what.** Essence Flare is the prime answer to Goblin Lackey, but that isn't the test for bringing it in: it comes in against any deck with one- and two-toughness creatures that matter, and especially against decks that plan to pressure you with many small threats, where three one-mana removal spells are worth more than the cards they replace. Back to Basics is for manlands, Kor Haven and the other utility lands that beat a counter deck, and for greedy mana bases — Terrageddon, GAT, Hermit FEB, Replenish — where it is a lock rather than a tax. Annul comes in by target count, one copy per three or four artifact and enchantment targets in their 75. The blasts are for the red decks, not for decks that merely contain red. Brain Freeze comes in with the plan, never alone.

**Habits.**

- I try not to go down two cantrips. It is tempting to read Opt, Peek and Impulse as cards that do nothing and cut them to make room for silver bullets, but they are the land count: cut three or four and the deck stops finding its answers, its Dreadnoughts, its land drops and the sideboard cards you just brought in. If the ins outnumber the acceptable outs, I would rather bring fewer cards in.

- One row per deck, split only where the deck itself splits into variants. Daze counts are defaults: adjust them for the play or the draw and for what you see at the table.

- A plan's cards come in packages. A Freeze plan trims Dreadnoughts and brings the Freezes together; a jam plan keeps all four with Daze and Peek. The awkward middle — four Dreadnoughts and no Daze — does neither.

**Sideboard against their sideboard, not their main deck.** This is one of the most played and most successful decks in the format, and most opponents who register a 75 have spent slots on it. Expect dedicated answers in most sideboards: artifact removal, red [Blasts]{Pyroblast}, Meddling Mage, Xantid Swarm, Gaea's Blessing against the Freeze plan. The pattern is the same everywhere: they cut their expensive cards, add one-mana answers, and stop tapping out. So build the row for the deck you will face in game two, not the one you saw in game one — the specifics are in each matchup entry, and the census chapter below lists what exists by color. **Overboarding.** A tool for the spot where you want Dreadnought to play like a three-of but keep the fourth copy in the deck: go to sixty-one or sixty-two instead of cutting. I use it only in the mirror, where library size is also a resource. Against aggro, dilution costs more than the fourth cantrip does.
