## How the deck plays

You only have to be one turn faster than your opponent. Everything about sequencing follows from deciding, early, which turn that is.

**Three modes.** Most games, and nearly all post-board games, are one of these three, and most sideboarding is choosing between them. Jam is the proactive mode: you present the threat and make them answer it. Freeze is the reactive one: you answer their threats and win off their library. Build-up sits between the two: you trade your interaction for their key cards until your hand has outgrown theirs, then commit once.

| Mode | Against | The turn | What you hold | What you cut |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jam | Low-interaction aggro, fast combo | Dreadnought by turn two to four with whatever protection the hand allows; the race is the whole game | Enablers, Daze and Foil | Flash, Thwart, Freeze |
| Build-up | Heavy interaction with a slow clock | Turn five or later: hit land drops, hold Gush and the Flash flashback, cast Portent so its draw lands after your discard step, go to ten or eleven cards against their seven, then commit once with everything | Gush, Flash, Foil for the commit turn | A Dreadnought or two, so the first one goes the distance |
| Freeze | Decks with ten or more answers to a 12/12 after boarding, or no counters and a lot of card draw | When the storm is there and a Stifle for their reshuffle (Gaea's Blessing) is in hand | Charms, Gush, the Freezes | Most of the Dreadnoughts; some stay as action they must answer |

**The hand-size edge.** Your opponent can hold seven cards. Fact or Fiction and Standstill are worth less to a player already holding seven: whatever they draw past that, they discard. If you win over several turns they cash those in; if you win in one Dreadnought cycle they cannot have the mana for all their draw and all their answers at once. So against control you leave Gush in hand and Flash in the yard until the turn you go, then draw to ten and play through.

{cards: Fact or Fiction | Standstill}

**Mana on the interaction turn.** Gush on the commit turn costs you nothing you need that turn, returns two Islands to pitch to Foil, and leaves you with the mana for Counterspell. A sample line from a stalled board at five lands each: Portent on your turn, its draw arriving after your discard step, flash back Flash of Insight at their end step, untap and draw to ten, Dreadnought, they counter, you counter back, they counter again, Gush and pitch-Foil to win the fight, then Charm the Dreadnought with its trigger on the stack, which also takes it out of reach of their Swords. You end with a phased-out Dreadnought, four lands and seven cards; they end with five lands and four cards. That is usually enough to protect it the next turn.

**When to commit.** Use your cantrips before committing unless you are under a clock. Count their answers: Peek is in the deck for exactly this. Stifle is the enabler, Vision Charm is the enabler that also protects, and Counterspell has three jobs — protect the combo, buy tempo on their development, and, against combo decks, stop their win condition — that you choose between every turn.

**Why the cantrips come first.** The combo costs two cards before it does anything: a Dreadnought and an enabler. If they answer the Dreadnought, you have handed them a two-for-one, and the deck has little to win those cards back — Gush and Flash of Insight, and not much else. Aim for the first Dreadnought you cast to be the last. So when the hand holds the combo and an Impulse, cast the Impulse and wait a turn. In that turn they draw one card, and it is rarely the answer; the Impulse, looking at four, very likely finds the Foil, the Counterspell or the Charm that makes the Dreadnought stick. Waiting improves your hand much more than theirs. The exceptions are a clock, and a lock piece — a Meddling Mage, a Sphere of Resistance — that is about to land and shut the combo off.

**The clock is two swings.** A Dreadnought kills in two attacks, so the commit turn has to come at least two turns before they would kill you, and that takes combat math. They can add creatures and race your two attacks with theirs, or chump-block to stretch your clock to three turns. Within that limit, go as late as you safely can: every draw step makes the hand better.

**Answers on the stack and answers on the battlefield.** Most blue decks, the mirror included, hold far more counters than ways to remove a resolved 12/12. A Dreadnought that lands before their counter defense is ready — early, or while they are tapped out — is much harder for them to deal with than one cast into open mana. That is the case for an early jam against a blue deck.

**Your draws are better than theirs.** With seventeen lands and eleven cantrips, more of your draws are spells, and most of your spells draw, counter or protect. A control or midrange deck plays more lands and has less selection. So a counter that trades with their key threat — a Terravore, an Exalted Angel — can buy you several draw steps, each worth more than theirs, until the hand has more protection than they have answers. And a Swords on your Dreadnought gives you twelve life, which buys the time for the next one.
