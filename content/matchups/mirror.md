### Dreadnought Mirror

{cards: Phyrexian Dreadnought | Brain Freeze | Annul | Powder Keg}

{cards: Stifle | Vision Charm | Chain of Vapor | Dominate}

**Verdict.** Even. It is a blue mirror in the old style — two players with full hands passing the turn — and the people who enjoy that enjoy nothing more. Game one is favorable for this list against mirrors without a main-deck Brain Freeze, which is the main reason the Freeze is main. Post-board it is a mill game on both sides, long and grinding, and the player with more patience usually wins it.

**What they do.** The same deck, in one of four builds: the stock list with Misdirection, the AK build, the Freeze-main build, or a jam list with no Brain Freeze, like Dave Kaplan's. Find out which as early as you can — a Misdirection, an Accumulated Knowledge or an early unprotected jam tells you — because the build decides how much the Dreadnought matters and whether a Freeze is coming.

**The golden rule.** Usually the first player to move loses. It isn't true every time, but it is the right posture, and the mindset to be in from the first turn: this is a mill game and a resource game before it is a Dreadnought game. Learn to play it fast, in paper and online. Mill games run long, and in paper some matches time out before game two is over — which is one more reason winning game one matters so much here.

**What the matchup hinges on: not going to discard.** With neither player committing to the board, each turn is a land drop and a cantrip, and the player who can't make the land drop, or who casts the card-advantage spell — a Gush, a flashback Flash, an Accumulated Knowledge for two or more — is the player who goes to discard at the end of the turn. Discarding a Flash of Insight is fine; discarding anything else is a card lost. Portent is the best way to defer the problem by a turn, since its draw arrives after your discard step. While you cantrip, the priorities are in order:

1. Early, make the land drops and acquire permission and interaction — Counterspell, Daze, a Chain of Vapor — for the early jam the opponent may try. Jamming early yourself is a way to win this game when you have the nuts, or when a Peek shows the coast is clear; but if it backfires you usually lose, because every Dreadnought you commit is a card down, every Foil you pitch-cast (or Misdirection, in the lists that run it) is a card down, and a jam protected by a Foil that meets a Counterspell and a Daze is two cards down for nothing.

2. Once the interaction is adequate, keep making land drops and acquire the storm cards: Brain Freeze, Vision Charm, and Stifle as protection for the storm trigger.

This is also the matchup where the bottom of your library matters most. Every Opt, Impulse and Flash puts cards there in a known order, the game is long enough that you will draw them, and nothing shuffles unless you choose to Portent yourself and miss. Arrange them deliberately — the Dreadnought you don't want yet below the Charm you do — and keep the map in your head or in your notes; knowing your next ten draws is worth more than most spells in the deck.

Every Vision Charm counts, so early in the game use Stifle as the enabler for a Dreadnought and save the Charms. Once all eight Dreadnoughts have been neutralized, the player who resolves more Vision Charms at the other's library usually wins — especially before sideboarding, when neither side has the extra Freezes.

**Game one: Dreadnought and Keg are not dead cards.** They are the cards you play when you are about to go to discard. If the end of turn would cost you a card anyway, jam the Dreadnought, or land the Keg — which then sits at one for the rest of the game as the answer to their Dreadnought. Gush is the easiest way to set these turns up: both players on seven, your mana well developed, and now they have to answer a threat. Trading answers one for one is fine, and you can also let the Dreadnought go: their resource for a card you were about to discard is a trade you take.

When you jam, they may respond by jamming their own Dreadnought, to block yours and trade, or to race by Charming yours on your upkeep. Chain of Vapor lets you interact with their Dreadnought on your own terms — let it resolve, set up the turn when you play your hand down to size, and Chain it then — at the cost of making their Vision Charm a relevant answer to the Chain.

And the Dreadnought has one more job here: it is the best way to start a lethal Freeze chain on your own turn. Jam it, protect it on the stack, and every spell in that fight — yours and theirs — is storm; when the dust settles you have the count, the mana you floated, and they have spent their counters on the Dreadnought rather than holding them for the Freeze. Do this when the resources under you are enough to build the whole chain and still beat their Stifles, not before.

**Counter the Dreadnought or the enabler?** It depends. Countering the Dreadnought is the default and the conservative choice. When you are heavy on countermagic, or they can't pay for two enablers, there is value in fighting over the enabler instead: the Dreadnought dies to its own trigger and the enablers come out of their hand. Every spell in that fight lengthens the storm chain their Brain Freeze is waiting for, so weigh it. And an enabler drawn out is rarely wasted: every one they spend defending a Dreadnought is one not milling you and not Stifling your storm trigger.

**After sideboarding.** Both sides have Annul and Chain, and most opponents board out some Dreadnoughts, so it takes very unlucky draws to be beaten by the 12/12: don't play scared of it. One caution: some mirrors board in Dominate, and a Dreadnought you were happy to lose becomes a 12/12 attacking you. A Counterspell for the Dominate, a Charm in response, or a Chain on the stolen Dreadnought afterwards is worth holding when you've seen the card or suspect the build. The game is about not getting milled before you mill them. A Freeze on their end step after they tap out uses their cantrips as your storm; hold a Stifle for their Stifle on your trigger, and bait it with the first Freeze if you hold two. When their partial Freeze is on the stack and you need a card that is about to be milled — your own Freezes most of all — a Flash from the graveyard in response takes it out of the library before the mill resolves.

With Freeze the plan on both sides, it is often correct to take the draw when you win the roll: you get an extra card in hand, and with the sixty-first card in the deck you also start with more cards in your library than an opponent on sixty, so when both sides trade off all their resources, they are the first to draw from an empty library.

**Sideboarding (61):** −1 Dreadnought, −2 Daze, −1 Peek, −1 Keg / +3 Annul, +2 Brain Freeze, +1 Chain.

**Why these.** Annul is the one-mana answer to their Dreadnought — it counters the artifact spell on cast, before the trigger fight, so no enabler ever matters — and to their Keg; it does nothing against the Freeze, which is an instant, and that is what the Stifles are for. The two extra Freezes make three, enough that one Stifle on your storm trigger doesn't end the plan, and enough to threaten the two-Freeze turn that mills forty-five after six other spells.

Daze leaves because the opponent isn't tapping out. Peek is the cantrip that goes: it selects nothing, and in a game this long the hand it shows is out of date by the turn you commit. Thwart stays: it is a hard counter, and in a game this long you usually hard-cast it for four mana rather than send three Islands back in a game decided by land drops and hand size. Like any counter, it stops only one storm copy of a Freeze, but it counters the Dreadnought, a Dominate or the card-advantage spell outright. Three Dreadnoughts stay as the action that forces answers and the turn-two window on the play. Keg leaves and the second Chain comes in. After sideboarding the Dreadnought is the smaller threat and the game is a long counter war, and Chain is the better card in it: one mana at instant speed, it answers their Dreadnought on your terms, after they have spent an enabler on it, sends back a stolen one, and, being an instant, adds to the storm count on the Freeze turn. Keg has to be cast into open counters and Annul, then sits on the table telling them what it does, and its best setting is the Dreadnought you are no longer afraid of.

And the deck goes to sixty-one rather than cutting a sixth card: in a mill race, library size is a resource, and one card of library is cheaper than any card in the sixty.
