### Terrageddon

{cards: Terravore | Armageddon | Sylvan Library | Mox Diamond}

{cards: Weathered Wayfarer | Werebear | Rishadan Port | Swords to Plowshares}

This entry assumes the Naya build, the common one; 4C Terrageddon, which adds blue for Meddling Mage, is covered at the end with its own row.

**Verdict.** Slightly unfavorable. Game one is close, and can lean your way when they are slow to build a clock; after sideboarding their pressure and the number of answers they bring tilt it toward them.

**Counter Sylvan Library.** It is their biggest development threat, in every game of the match: every turn it stays is a turn they get further ahead on cards and closer to the answers they need. Counterspell it in game one; after sideboarding, Annul it. Don't pitch a [Foil]{Foil} at it, though: it is a strong card, but not worth three of yours, and a second Library is close to a dead card in their hand while the first is on the battlefield — Foil the first and the second does its job anyway (see [Foil](#foil)).

**What they do.** A Naya midrange deck built around two cards: Terravore, which grows with every land in every graveyard, and Armageddon, which turns a board of creatures into a game that is very hard to come back from. Around those, the engine is Sylvan Library, which is both card advantage and card selection — the best card of their top three every turn, and more when they will pay the life for it. Mox Diamond is the acceleration: it fixes their three colors, puts lands in the graveyard for Terravore and threshold, and survives their own Armageddon. Weathered Wayfarer is the toolbox, fetching a land whenever they are behind on lands — Wasteland, Rishadan Port, Treetop Village, City of Traitors, a cycling land — and it is how they keep Wasteland and Port coming — though against you only Port matters, since every land you play is a basic Island and Wasteland has nothing to hit. The threat suite varies from list to list: Nimble Mongoose and Werebear, which both turn on with threshold, Call of the Herd, an Anurid Brushhopper, and Wild Mongrel in some versions. Expect four Swords to Plowshares in the main deck, and both Naturalize and Disenchant alongside them.

**What the matchup hinges on.** Their clock against your sculpting. Sylvan Library makes them hard to out-card, though in a longer game this deck is fully capable of it; what you can't do is jam into a hand full of answers. So the game is about finding a hand that protects the Dreadnought before their creatures and their Library add up — and if the game goes long, your cantrips, Gush and Flash keep pace with the Library. Armageddon is weaker against you than against most of the field — you operate on two lands, and Gush saves two of them — but Rishadan Port still taps the mana you need on the commit turn.

**Game one.** Sculpt a protected hand, and judge their clock to know how long you have. If they are slow to develop pressure, or can't find threshold, you have time to wait for Counterspell plus Charm and the land drops to cast both; if Mongoose and Werebear are already threshold-sized, you have to go sooner, with what you have.

Once they reach Armageddon mana, keep Gush ready: Gush in response to Armageddon returns two Islands to your hand before it resolves, and you rebuild faster than they do. Chain of Vapor on a Mox Diamond is a Stone Rain — they have to replay it and pitch another land — and it is best just before you jam. Mind the copy: they can sacrifice a land to point it at your Dreadnought, so have a counter for the copy or the Dreadnought not yet on the table. Powder Keg at zero kills every Mox Diamond and every Elephant token without touching your Dreadnought. Charm in response to Swords, not pre-emptively.

Stifle has plenty to do here: Rishadan Port in your upkeep or on the commit turn, the sacrifice of an Aura of Silence or a Seal of Cleansing, a Weathered Wayfarer activation, a Windswept Heath when they are short of a color. Keg has more settings than zero: two kills a Werebear, three a Terravore, zero an animated Treetop Village as well as the Moxes and the tokens.

**After sideboarding.** They bring more answers to the Dreadnought: Aura of Silence in most sideboards, more artifact removal, Reprisal in some lists, and the red [Blasts]{Pyroblast} to fight you on the stack — expect one on the Counterspell that protects your Dreadnought, and hold a Foil for it. Some bring Ray of Revelation for your Back to Basics, more likely in game three once they have seen it; it is narrow, since it does nothing to the Dreadnought, and most prefer answers that hit both. Many pilots go further and cut Armageddon and Terravore against you altogether: they lower their curve, keep mana up on your turn, and bring in Hidden Gibbons, which becomes a 4/4 the first time you cast an instant — and a deck this full of instants can rarely avoid casting one. Annul it on cast, before it can turn. With fewer lands in their graveyard and more answers in their hand, the game becomes pressure plus disruption against your sculpting.

{cards: Aura of Silence | Hidden Gibbons | Reprisal | Pyroblast}

Your plan is to control the game until you can either resolve and protect Back to Basics or jam a protected Dreadnought. Back to Basics attacks their very greedy mana base, and it also overloads their artifact and enchantment removal, so the Naturalizes and the Auras aren't all pointed at the Dreadnought.

**Sideboarding:** −1 Brain Freeze, −1 Thwart, −2 Daze, −1 Flash / +3 Annul, +2 Back to Basics.

**Why these.** Annul is the answer to their Libraries, their Auras and Hidden Gibbons: the Aura first, because it taxes the Dreadnought by two and then sacrifices as a Disenchant, then the Library, then a Mox — and Hidden Gibbons on cast ahead of all of them, on the opponents who bring it, because once it resolves your first instant turns it into a 4/4. Back to Basics is far better than Web here: it locks Gemstone Mine, Rith's Grove, City of Traitors and the painlands, leaving them Forests, Plains, Windswept Heath and Mox Diamond, and Back to Basics also turns Rishadan Port into a land that taps once and never untaps; Web stops only the lands with activated abilities — Port, Treetop Village, the cycling lands. The second Chain stays in the sideboard: resetting a Mox is cute, but not worth a card that hands them a copy to point at your Dreadnought.

Brain Freeze is dead against a deck that doesn't draw itself out. Daze leaves because they play draw-go with Swords up and rarely tap out into it. Thwart leaves to make room: Annul does its job against the cards that matter here — Library, Aura, Gibbons — for one mana and no Islands. One Flash goes because their deck is fast enough early to punish a slow card, and one stays for the long game that follows a resolved Back to Basics. Keg stays for the Moxes and the Elephant tokens.

{card: Meddling Mage}
**4C Terrageddon.** The same deck with blue added for Meddling Mage in the main, and that makes the matchup unfavorable: a Mage naming Phyrexian Dreadnought, or the enabler you are short of, shuts the combo off for as long as it lives, and it comes down early enough to name the card before you can protect it. Counter it on cast when you can; after sideboarding, Essence Flare kills it in two upkeeps, and a Keg already sitting at two kills it at once. The rest of the plan is the Naya one. Their mana is worse than Naya's — to fit four colors they cut the cycling lands for more multicolor lands — so Back to Basics hurts them even more.

**Sideboarding (4C):** −1 Brain Freeze, −1 Thwart, −2 Daze, −2 Flash, −1 Peek / +3 Annul, +2 Back to Basics, +2 Flare.

**Why these, against 4C.** Flare is the answer to the Mage that you can cast at sorcery speed on your own terms. Two Flares come in, and Peek makes room, so Annul stays at three: a Mage is the card that matters more than any one Library or Aura, but their Libraries, Auras and Moxes are still the targets that decide the long game. Both Flashes leave: the game turns on answering the Mage early, not on the long game.
