### Landstill

{cards: Standstill | Humility | Fact or Fiction | Decree of Justice}

{cards: Mishra's Factory | Faerie Conclave | Kor Haven | Swords to Plowshares}

**Verdict.** Even, and it stays even after sideboarding: Meddling Mage and Exalted Angel are hard to answer, and they give Landstill enough of a proactive plan to win some of the time.

**What they do.** The most popular control deck in the format. It answers threats both on the stack and once they have resolved, while it builds up a big advantage in lands and cards, and every answer is trying to trade one card for one or more of yours. That is why it doesn't play Foil, and it doesn't play enough Islands for Gush, Daze or Thwart, so it usually has no free spells at all — which makes the first couple of turns its weakest.

The removal is four Swords to Plowshares, Disenchant, Seal of Cleansing, Powder Keg, Wrath of God, and Aura of Silence, usually from the sideboard. The counters, around eight of them, are Mana Leak, Counterspell, Prohibit, Absorb and Forbid. Watch their open mana: in the early turns they often struggle to find the mana for Counterspell, and in the late turns a Mana Leak can rot in their hand if you can always pay three.

Most lists also play a lock piece in the main deck: Humility. Under it your Dreadnought is a 1/1 that they can chump-block with anything, and a resolved Humility is extremely hard to come back from. Treat it as the proactive card it is: something they can do to lock the game out.

The main plan is the namesake card, Standstill. They have a deck full of lands and answers, and two win conditions, neither of which needs them to cast a spell: their manlands, Mishra's Factory and Faerie Conclave, and the 1/1 Soldier tokens from a cycled Decree of Justice. Cycling Decree is an activated ability followed by a triggered one, and neither breaks Standstill. So they park a Standstill and attack you with lands and tokens.

Their card advantage is mostly Fact or Fiction, fired at the end of your turn to spend their mana, and a broken Standstill.

**What the matchup hinges on.** Time. They need mana to cast several spells a turn, and you have free counters; the longer the game goes, the more of their lands and cards they get to use, and the harder it becomes for you.

Their clock, on the other hand, is extremely slow. They like to pass with mana up, so they stretch the game out rather than attack with their manlands from the start: expect no aggression before turn six or seven, and then only a couple of points of damage a turn. You have a lot more time to sculpt here than against almost anything else in the format. What runs short is not your life but the window before their mana lets them answer everything at once.

**Mulligans.** That slow clock changes the keep. Your priority is to make your land drops for the first four or five turns, so keep hands that can do that — a five-land seven included.

**Game one.** You are the beatdown. Present a Dreadnought with two or three layers of protection before they have the mana and the cards to throw three or four answers at it.

To do that, your commit turn usually uses Gush or Flash of Insight to go up in cards. For example: with seven cards in hand, Gush among them, float two mana, Gush up to ten, go off and drop to eight, fight their Counterspell with a Charm or a Counterspell of your own and pass with seven. Ideally they don't have the lands to Fact or Fiction at your end step, and a Standstill now does them no good, because you have the bigger threat on the table. You will be up on cards next turn, and when they try to interact at your upkeep, you can fight them off with Foils and Vision Charms.

The cards of theirs you need to answer:

- **Fact or Fiction.** If you want to stay in a long game, counter it, or you fall far behind on cards.
- **Standstill.** Counter it for the same reason. Once one resolves, the game gets better for them every turn, so at some point you have to break it. The best time is their end step, when they already hold seven cards: they draw three and have to discard three. They still get plenty of selection, but it is the smallest of the blows.
- **Humility.** Counter it on sight.
- **A late Decree of Justice cycled for three or more Soldiers.** Stifle the trigger that makes the tokens, not the cycling: Stifling the cycling activation only counters the draw.

**Splitting their Fact or Fiction.** When one resolves, you make the piles, and that is harder than it looks because it gives you a lot of choice. The easiest way to think about it: most players take the bigger pile no matter what. So pick the two cards you least want them to have and put those in the smaller pile; if they take the small pile anyway, you have usually made a good split. Two other ways to build the piles. Lands against spells: spells are hard to deploy without lands, and lands do nothing without spells, so make them choose. And put two copies of the same card in the same pile: most cards don't do much in multiples. Split most piles three and two. Going four and one makes sense only in the rare case where one card is backbreaking and you have little to answer it with. Remember that they don't know which cards they need to beat your hand, so a three-two split with the two best cards in the small pile will, more often than not, take those cards out of their hand and their deck for good.

If game one goes long because they never present a threat, remember that you can also sculpt a win with the main-deck Brain Freeze — especially after a long counter fight over a Dreadnought late in the game. Picture the turn: Gush two or three times, go off, Counterspell, Foil, Foil, Flash of Insight once or twice, then Vision Charm them and Brain Freeze. That is likely half their deck or more.

Once you are on the mill plan, their own card draw helps you: a broken Standstill takes three cards off their library, and a Fact or Fiction five. When you are well placed to deliver a Brain Freeze kill, let those spells resolve and let them do the math for you.

A common pitfall: you can't get out from under a Standstill by Stifling its trigger. Casting the Stifle is a spell too, so Standstill triggers again, and they still draw three.

Powder Keg is a great tool here. Keep it at zero most of the time — after sideboarding, at two when a Meddling Mage has to die. At zero it threatens every animated manland and every Soldier token, so they stop attacking with their manlands and think twice before cycling Decree. If they can't answer it, they will eventually start sending a single manland at your life total, one they can afford to lose to the Keg. Judge then whether it is time to trade, or whether that clock is too small to matter.

Kor Haven shows up in some lists, and it fogs your Dreadnought every turn. Your main-deck answers are Stifling the activation, committing a second Dreadnought, or the Vision Charm trick when their only white mana comes from Plains: turn their Plains into Mountains in your beginning-of-combat step, and they can't activate Kor Haven when you attack. That isn't a reliable plan, though, since they play Coastal Tower and Adarkar Wastes as well.

Phyrexian Furnace is particularly annoying: it makes your Flash of Insight worse in a matchup where you want that long-term selection and card advantage. It isn't worth a Counterspell, though.

**After sideboarding.** They bring Annul, a more efficient answer to the Dreadnought; Meddling Mage; sometimes Exalted Angel; and more artifact and enchantment removal: extra Seals of Cleansing and Aura of Silence.

{cards: Annul | Meddling Mage | Exalted Angel | Aura of Silence}

You bring Back to Basics, because their mana base is greedy and full of nonbasics, among them their manlands and sometimes a Kor Haven. A resolved Back to Basics is often a big advantage, cutting off their mana and their manland plan at once. Annul is for Standstill and Humility, and Essence Flare for Meddling Mage. And you bring Brain Freeze as a second way to win: it overloads their Meddling Mages, which now have two win conditions to name instead of one, and it gives you a win condition their counters struggle with — the storm copies aren't cast, so each counter stops only one of them — to fire late in the game, after a fight over a Dreadnought has raised the storm count.

Back to Basics and the Freeze plan force them to play more proactively, and they let you take a more controlling approach in the sideboard games. The ideal is a game where they are stuck holding several Swords to Plowshares while you set up the mill.

**Sideboarding:** −2 Dreadnought, −1 Stifle, −2 Daze, −2 Foil, −1 Peek / +2 Annul, +2 Back to Basics, +2 Flare, +2 Brain Freeze.

**Why these.** Daze leaves because in a long game they will very often have the mana to pay for it. Two Foils leave because Foil is card disadvantage, and this is a game about cards. Two Dreadnoughts leave because this is a longer game and you won't be under pressure early: you have the time to find one, and you want to avoid jamming several times, which is card disadvantage too. A Stifle goes with them: there are still good targets, but not enough to want four, and you don't expect them to bring a Brain Freeze plan of their own, so you won't need Stifle for a storm trigger. Peek makes room.

Thwart stays: it is free, and unlike Daze it doesn't care whether they can pay. The second Chain of Vapor stays in the sideboard. Two Annuls are enough for Standstill and Humility.
