## Card analysis: the engine and the utility

### Gush (4)

{card: Gush}
Free card advantage that also pays for Foil — but only if the cards stay in your hand. Gush is how the deck refills after it has spent its hand, and a Gush cast into a full hand you aren't about to unload just draws two and discards two at end of turn: selection, not advantage. The question before every Gush is whether the two cards survive your discard step. Hold it. With five cards and no land drop you can Gush without discarding. With fewer than five, Gush is your refill: save it for the turn you have no land drop and no cantrip to cast, so it isn't spent on a job another card could do. Against control it waits for the commit turn; against land destruction it waits for the commit turn too, because returning Islands into Thermokarst is how you lose. Its drawback is smallest on the turn you already have all the mana you need.

**How to play it.** Gush is the reason the deck functions on two lands, and it is easy to cast wrong. The short rules for this deck:

- Cast it in your main phase, floating the mana. Tap both Islands for UU, return them to Gush, draw two, replay one Island as your land drop, and spend the floated mana on the cantrips or the Dreadnought. You have paid nothing: the mana is still there, the land drop is still made, and the hand is two cards bigger.

- Gush guarantees a land drop. On a turn with no land in hand, Gush returns two and you play one. That is why a Gush hand with two Islands is a keep and why missing drops is cheap.

- Gush is what lets you make every land drop and still hold Islands for Foil. The returned Island that isn't replayed is the pitch, and a hand with an Island in it is a hand Thermokarst and Armageddon can't empty.

- Gush at their end step is almost never right. Returning two Islands at instant speed when you have nothing to spend the floated mana on throws away the mana and gains nothing you wouldn't have had next turn. The exceptions are real but rare: in response to land destruction, to save the targeted Islands; in response to a threat, when the two cards might be a Foil or a Daze you can cast now; and under Choke or [Stasis]{Stasis}, where two tapped Islands returned to hand are an untapped land drop and a Foil pitch. Hard-casting it for five mana is right in almost no game this deck plays.

- Gush is a free spell for storm. Two Gushes on a Freeze turn are plus two storm, plus four cards for nothing — you still make only one land drop a turn — which is why the Freeze lines in this deck start with them.

### Portent (4)

{card: Portent}
Eleven cantrips are the land count, and Portent is the best of them: a sorcery whose draw arrives at the opponent's upkeep, after your discard to hand size. Most of the time you cast it on yourself; pointing it at the opponent is the rare exception, covered below. Being a sorcery has a cost the instants don't pay: it spends mana on your own turn, so there are turns when the right play is to hold it until you can cast it and still hold up your interaction. A turn-two Portent with a Counterspell in hand turns the Counterspell off for a turn; the same Portent on turn three, with the mana for both, costs nothing.

**How to play it.** The hardest card in the deck to play well, because of the delayed draw: the card arrives after your discard step and during their turn, where a Counterspell or a Stifle is live but a Dreadnought is exposed to discard before you can cast it — not to Duress, which can't take a creature, but to a Cabal Therapy, and Therapy players who see Islands name Phyrexian Dreadnought in the blind.

The ordering follows from that. The top card is the Portent draw; the second is your next natural draw; the third is the one after. Put the instant you want on their turn on top and the card you want to cast before they can discard it — the Dreadnought, the Freeze — second, so it arrives in your draw step and is cast before they untap. If all three are bad, shuffle.

The slow trip is the other half of the card: a Portent cast with seven in hand draws you to eight on their upkeep, after your discard, so against discard-to-hand-size and against your own Gush turns it is card advantage the discard step never sees. Beyond that: hold it when you don't yet know whether you need a land, when you're about to Gush and don't want to discard, or when you already know the top cards.

**In paper, remember the draw.** The delayed draw is the easiest trigger in the deck to forget, and the best players in the world forget it too. Make it a habit: when you cast Portent, put a die on top of your library, or move the top card of your library face down beside it, and draw when you see the reminder. Remembering is your job, not your opponent's; if their upkeep passes and you haven't drawn, the card is gone. Online, the client draws it for you.

**On the opponent.** Pointing Portent at the other player is rare, and the test is whether you are ahead. These are the situations that qualify: they are low on cards, so their next three draws are most of what they have left; you have seen their hand with Peek and know they hold no answer to the Dreadnought, so the only answer is on top of their library; they have missed a land drop and the top three decide whether they recover; or an [Oath]{Oath of Druids} is about to trigger and you need to know whether a Terravore is waiting.

Then you have three tools. Give them one bad card: put the worst of the three on top and leave the rest. Give them three bad cards: if none of the three is what they need, leave them all in the worst order, and against a mana-screwed opponent that means every nonland stays on top. Or shuffle, when the three are good and the odds of three random cards being worse are better than certain.

A fetchland or a cantrip of their own undoes your arrangement, so leave the stack only when they can't shuffle it away, and shuffle them instead when they can. With two Portents in hand on the same turn, cast one on each player: a second Portent on your own library looks at the three cards you just arranged and tells you nothing, while the same spell on theirs is information and a draw step taken away. Portent them, then Charm them, is the last version — arrange their best three on top and mill four — and like the Charm on their own Portent it is a play for when you are ahead.

### Opt (4), Impulse (2), Peek (1)

{cards: Opt | Impulse | Peek}
Three instants that do three different jobs. Opt filters between classes of card: when the question is "do I need a land or a spell?", scry one answers it cheaply. Impulse digs four deep and finds a specific card — the Dreadnought that closes a race, the enabler for the Dreadnought in hand — which is why it is the last cantrip to leave against aggro and the one with Flash of Insight that you reach for when you know what you are looking for. One thing the printed Impulse gets wrong: under current Oracle wording the three cards you don't take go on the bottom of your library in any order — no shuffle. That makes Impulse a third tool, with Opt and Flash, for arranging the bottom of your deck, and against an opponent who still plays it as a shuffle you know more about both libraries than they do. Peek selects nothing; it is the deck's information spell: against counters, Swords decks and combo it tells you whether the jam is safe; against aggro and discard decks its information is worth little and it goes first.

**When to cast them.** The default is the end of the opponent's turn, with the mana having sat untapped as a Counterspell threat all turn and nothing having needed it; you give up nothing and see the card before your own draw.

Two exceptions. The first is when the card is needed now: digging for a land drop in your own main phase, or for a Foil before they untap. The second is the moment the opponent taps out. Cantrip right then, in their main phase, rather than waiting for the end step: having tapped out they are unlikely to have another play, so the end step gains nothing, and the cantrip might find a Daze — which, with them tapped out and a spell on the stack, is the best card you can draw.

Beyond that, hold Opt as storm for a Freeze turn, or to tuck a card you've just seen off Portent; and Peek goes first among the instants at their end step when you plan to Portent them on your turn, because knowing their hand decides what to leave on top.

### Flash of Insight (2)

{card: Flash of Insight}
A two-for-one that survives counters and discard, because the second half lives in the graveyard where Duress can't reach it. It is slow, so it leaves against anything fast, and it is the best card in the deck in a long game.

**How to play it.** The graveyard is where this card belongs, and the first job is getting it there cheaply. It is the best card in the deck to pitch to Foil and the best card to discard to hand size — nothing is lost, and the flashback is waiting. Short of a free route, cycle it: cast it from hand for X=1 with spare mana, or spend the mana a little aggressively, unless you are holding it as the Foil pitch.

Then find the turn for the flashback. That is where the card earns its slot: for 1U and the graveyard, selection deep enough to go up in cards on the one turn that decides the game, cast at instant speed — in response to the spell you need to answer, exile the yard and find the Counterspell; or at their end step before Phyrexian Furnace or Withered Wretch can exile the yard.

Against heavy discard it does something no other card does: the best card is safer in your library, where a big Flash reaches it, than in your hand where a Duress or a Therapy takes it. And in the mirror it is a rescue: when a partial Brain Freeze is on the stack and the card you need — your own Freeze, the last Charm — is in the part of the library about to be milled, flash back Flash in response and take it before the mill resolves.

Charm your own library to grow the yard and the X. And remember there are two. The first flashback doesn't have to go to maximum depth unless you need one specific card right now; leaving some cards in the graveyard makes the second Flash bigger.

**The bottom of your library is yours to arrange.** Opt, Impulse and Flash all put cards on the bottom, and in a long game you will draw that part of the deck. A big Flash late in a game can stack your entire remaining library. So never put cards back at random: decide the order, and remember it. Knowing which cards are in which part of the deck, and what you are about to draw, is an enormous edge — and it starts at the mulligan, with the card you bottomed. The deck has one shuffle effect, Portent's, and only when you choose it; if you never shuffle, the information holds for the whole game. Online, keep notes. In paper, remember the key cards and roughly where they are — the bottom five, the bottom ten, the bottom twenty.

### Chain of Vapor (1 main, 1 side)

{card: Chain of Vapor}
One mana that takes back a permanent, and the measure of it is how much they paid to put that permanent there. Its best targets are the ones that cost two or more cards to deploy: their Dreadnought in the mirror, a Mox Diamond that cost them a land, a Graveborn Muse or a Phyrexian Arena that was Ritualed out, anything that arrived off a Show and Tell. The best of all is a reanimated creature: a Chain on the Akroma or the Phantom Nishoba they just Reanimated sends it back to a hand it can't be cast from, and to try again they need another discard outlet to get it into the graveyard and another reanimation spell to bring it back — and if that spell is Reanimate, the life as well. Bouncing any of these is a two-for-one that also costs them the tempo. Beyond that it is the only post-resolution answer to [Stasis]{Stasis}, Confinement or Choke. On Freeze plans with no Dreadnought out it is one-sided. Shroud stops it: Argothian Enchantress, Nimble Mongoose and anything under Sterling Grove can't be targeted, and a creature protected by Mother of Runes is protected from blue.

{cards: Mox Diamond | Graveborn Muse | Phyrexian Arena | Akroma, Angel of Wrath}

**How to play it.** In response to a Tog or Shade pump, after the discard. On a Dryad or a Shapeshifter as a reset. On your own Dreadnought as a save, and on your own Keg or Back to Basics in response to the Disenchant. Whenever you Chain your own permanent, remember the copy is yours to make too: sacrifice an Island and point the copy at one of their permanents, and the save becomes a save plus a tempo play, at the cost of a land — worth it when you have Islands to spare.

The rule is to avoid casting it with your own Dreadnought on the table against a deck with lands to spare, because they will sacrifice one to copy it at your Dreadnought. When you have to anyway, you have three ways out. Recast the Dreadnought, which costs another enabler. Charm it in response to the copy, which costs a Charm. Or the best of the three, which people miss: the copy is a spell on the stack, and Counterspell, Foil, Daze or Thwart counters it like any other.

### Powder Keg (1)

{card: Powder Keg}
The settings are the card. 0 kills Moxes, Elephant and [Decree]{Decree of Justice} tokens, face-down morphs and an animated Factory or Treetop, and never touches your Dreadnought. 2 kills Dryad, Werebear, Mongrel, Meddling Mage, Sphere of Resistance, Grid, Altar, Argothian Enchantress through shroud, and Goblin Tinkerer before it untaps. 3 kills Terravore, Psychatog and Specter; 4 kills Negator. 1 kills every dork, Lackey, Swarm, [Welder]{Goblin Welder} and [Scroll]{Cursed Scroll} — and your Dreadnought, so the sequence is Keg, crack at one, then Dreadnought, or hold it at zero once the Dreadnought is down. Its activation is not a spell, so it works under Standstill; its counters are added at upkeep, so it works under [Stasis]{Stasis}; and it doesn't target, so shroud, protection and Mother of Runes save nothing from it.

**How to play it.** It does not hit enchantments; Annul and Chain do. The counter climbs through every mana value on the way up, and your own Dreadnought is at one: a Keg at one with your Dreadnought on the table is cracked only once the Dreadnought is already phased out — Charm first, let it resolve, then activate; otherwise crack it before you commit, or let it climb past.

The harder skill is not cracking it. A Keg sitting at the right number is a threat the opponent has to play around every turn: they hold the second Mage rather than cast it into a Keg at two, they keep the answer they'd rather deploy on your turn, they stop developing the board the Keg is pointed at.

Often the last possible moment is the right one, when the board has filled with the things it kills and nothing they do can take it away first. And often it isn't: there are turns when now is the time, the next permanent is the one that beats you, and waiting a turn for one more target is how the game is lost. There is no rule that covers both. What there is, is the habit of asking, every upkeep the Keg goes up, what cracking it now buys and what holding it threatens — and of remembering that an opponent who respects the threat is already paying for it.

### Brain Freeze (1 main, 2 side)

{card: Brain Freeze}
The plan B. It mills three cards per storm count. The storm ability triggers on cast and puts the copies on the stack when it resolves, so Counterspell on the original still lets every copy resolve, Misdirection moves only the original, and what stops it is Stifle on the storm trigger or a card that shuffles a graveyard back — Gaea's Blessing above all. Storm counts every spell cast by anyone that turn, countered or not. It is at its best against three kinds of deck, which overlap: decks with many answers to a Dreadnought, where the combo plan is the losing one; slow control decks that play a card-advantage game rather than a tempo game, where the long turns build your storm and nothing of theirs pressures you; and decks that draw a lot of cards through an engine — Attunement, Argothian Enchantress and Enchantress's Presence, Horn of Greed — and so thin their own library for you. Three copies matter for two reasons: discard exists, and one Freeze lost to a Duress shouldn't end the plan; and the turns that win are the ones where two Freezes go off together for a lethal mill that doesn't arrive too late in the game, which three copies make a realistic thing to assemble rather than a lucky one. It is at its worst against aggro, where it is a dead card, and against Gaea's Blessing decks, where every mill has to be played around with a Stifle held for the trigger, and with two or more Blessings in their deck the Stifles run out before the library does. Against Psychatog or Genesis decks it needs care: a non-lethal mill feeds them.

**Why one in the main.** Most lists give this slot to Misdirection. The Freeze is here for game one of the mirror: against a mirror with no main-deck Freeze, it is the only win in either 60 that doesn't need a Dreadnought to survive, and that makes game one favorable. The cost is a card that is dead against aggro, which is why it is the first card out in those rows — and Misdirection is dead in many of the same matchups: turning a burn spell onto a creature, or back at the opponent, is a play, but a poor one for the card it costs.

**The math.** Each Freeze mills three for itself and three for every spell cast before it that turn, by anyone. A second Freeze in the same turn counts the first, so the two together are worth more than double. Cards milled, by the number of spells already cast when the first Freeze goes on the stack:

Read it with the mana in mind: one per Charm, two per Freeze, and the rest of the storm from free spells and one-mana cantrips. Three Charms and nothing else, then two Freezes, is thirty-nine cards for seven mana; put a Gush and an Opt in front of them — five spells, three of them Charms — and the same two Freezes mill fifty-one for eight. That is the turn the whole plan is built to reach.

**Building the storm.** The tools, in the order you reach for them: Vision Charm, because it mills while it counts. The free spells — Gush every Island back to hand, and Daze, Foil and Thwart on anything, including your own spells, since a spell countered still counted. Flash of Insight from the graveyard, which is a spell for the storm and finds the next Freeze. The one-mana cantrips, which count for one and dig for the rest. Count the mana before you start: everything in the chain, the Freezes themselves, and the Stifle you are holding up for a Gaea's Blessing or for their own Freeze. A full chain usually needs either a lot of mana under you or very few cards left in their library.

**When to Freeze.** Their upkeep is the best time, because they are about to draw and have the least chance to respond; it is also when you have the fewest spells to count, since they won't cast their own to feed your storm, so the chain has to come entirely from your hand. Their end step uses the storm a big turn of theirs built for you, and carries a risk: in a mirror or against any deck with Freeze, they can Freeze you back in the same window, and then you draw first and lose. So a Freeze on their turn means holding Stifle for their Freeze, or deciding to gamble that they don't have it.

Some decks can make you draw at instant speed — Opportunity, Cephalid Coliseum — and against those there is no safe window; Freezing in response to their draw spell protects you a little. The third option is your own turn: use the Dreadnought as the storm-builder, jam it and let the counter war happen, then Freeze after the dust has settled, and they are the ones drawing next.

**How big.** Lethal is the aim. A non-lethal Freeze puts them on a clock and is often fine, but it is a gift to anything that uses a graveyard — Terravore, Psychatog, Reanimator, their own Flash of Insight and every other flashback spell — so against those, wait for lethal unless discard is about to take the Freezes out of your hand first. Freezing in two instalments across turns can work and can backfire for the same reason.

**Choosing a seat.** In a post-board mirror, where Freeze is the main line of attack on both sides, being on the draw is often the better seat. You get one extra card in hand, and with the mirror's sixty-one-card row you also start with more cards in your library than an opponent on sixty: when both sides trade off all their resources, they are the first to draw from an empty library. The extra card is worth more than the tempo when neither side is racing. Think before you take the play.

A bait Freeze draws their Stifle before the real one.

### 17 Islands

{card: Island}
Sixteen is a touch too few to Counterspell on turn two reliably; eighteen floods in a format where Gush and Daze already make extra Islands dead cards. Seventeen with eleven cantrips is the compromise, and it is what nearly every list plays. Against Elves and Goblins you mulligan aggressively for the Dreadnought, not for lands; the Islands and the cantrips find the second land.

**The xerox principle.** The land count only makes sense through it. A deck that wants to see a land every turn can get there two ways: play more lands, or play cheap cantrips that see more cards. The cantrip route is the xerox idea, and the rule of thumb is that two one-mana cantrips do the work of one land: they find the land drop when you need it and turn into spells when you don't, which an eighteenth Island never does.

This deck takes the idea nearly as far as it goes — seventeen Islands plus eleven cantrips plus four Gush is the land count of a twenty-two-land deck that rarely floods — and everything downstream depends on it: the one-land keeps, the two-land operating level, the Foil pitches, the Freeze storm counts, and the fact that a 60 with four Dreadnoughts and eight enablers finds them. The corollary is the one people get wrong at the sideboarding table, and it has its own rule in the sideboarding chapter: the cantrips are not do-nothing cards you cut to make room. They are the lands.
