## Card analysis: the combo and the counters

Every card in the 60 gets one entry here: why it is in the list, then how to play it. The two enablers are the deck's most flexible cards and the lines that win lost games come from knowing every mode, so their entries are the longest; the most complex lines depend on knowing what the card can do before the situation asks for it.

### Phyrexian Dreadnought (4)

{card: Phyrexian Dreadnought}
The threat, and the reason the deck exists: a one-mana 12/12 trampler that ends the game in two attacks. Cast it when you can protect it fully, or when you can't wait. Against control it is a card you hold until the hand is sculpted; against aggro it is the first thing you do. It is an artifact creature, so Duress can't take it and Vision Charm can phase it out of a Swords, an Edict, a Deed or a Wrath.

**How to play it.** The trigger is the whole card: when the Dreadnought enters, its ability goes on the stack, and you sacrifice it when the ability resolves unless you sacrifice twelve power. Everything happens while it waits. You respond with Stifle or Charm; the opponent responds too, with a [Blast]{Pyroblast} or a Counterspell on your enabler, which kills the Dreadnought, or with a Chain on the Dreadnought itself, which returns it to your hand: you keep the card, though an enabler already spent is gone — don't fight that one.

Cast the second copy to untap the first: sacrifice the tapped one to the new trigger, which matters for blocking and under [Stasis]{Stasis}. A Dreadnought that phases out and back in is not summoning sick, since phasing doesn't change control, so a Charmed Dreadnought attacks the turn it returns.

**Read the Oracle text, not the printed card.** The original printing says to bury the Dreadnought; the Oracle text, which is what the rules use, says you sacrifice it unless you sacrifice twelve power. The difference matters when an opponent steals it with the trigger on the stack. The trigger is still yours, and when it resolves it tells you to sacrifice the Dreadnought — but a player can't sacrifice a permanent they don't control, so nothing happens, and they keep a 12/12 that never has to be fed. Dominate in response to the trigger is the way this comes up. Chain your own Dreadnought in response to Swords or Vindicate when you have no Charm; it costs a turn and a second enabler but keeps the card. (A Keg at 1 can still be cracked with the Dreadnought out — see the Powder Keg entry.)

**After sideboarding.** When the plan is to go off on turn five or later, trim it: a hand with two Dreadnoughts and one enabler is a hand that two-for-ones itself. When the plan is Freeze, keep some: a Dreadnought on the stack is action the opponent has to answer, and either the answer comes and the way to a Freeze turn opens, or it doesn't and the 12/12 ends the game. An opponent who knows you have cut all four can prepare for the Freeze turn, or simply stop casting spells. The exception is a deck that has to cast spells and draw cards to function and has no way to win without doing so — Enchantress and Replenish, which can't win without Opalescence and can't mill you first — and there all four can go.

### Stifle (4)

{card: Stifle}
The enabler that does everything else. Half the format runs on activated and triggered abilities, and a one-mana instant that counters any of them is, in the right spots, a Stone Rain, a Disenchant, a removal spell or a counterspell for their best card. Stifle count scales with Dreadnought count when you trim, except where it has a job of its own: Blessing decks, Freeze mirrors, and anything built on abilities.

**Beyond the Dreadnought trigger.** The targets, roughly in order of how often they come up.

- Their removal. Seal of Cleansing is the white deck's second-commonest Dreadnought answer after Swords, and it is an activation: sacrifice the Seal, destroy the artifact. Stifle it and the Seal is gone, the Dreadnought isn't, and the sacrifice is already paid. Aura of Silence's second mode and Seal of Removal work the same way. So does their Powder Keg cracked at 1 with your Dreadnought on the table: Stifle keeps the Dreadnought and costs them the Keg.

- Their engines. Hermit Druid. Altar of Dementia, so the sacrificed [Devourer]{Phyrexian Devourer} is wasted. Recurring Nightmare, where they lose the sacrificed creature and get nothing back. Goblin Welder's exchange. Tradewind Rider. Pernicious Deed. Survival of the Fittest is a different case. You never want the card itself to resolve — it is too powerful — so counter it on the way down whenever you can. Once it is on the battlefield, though, Stifling its activation is rarely worth the Stifle: it costs them one green mana and a card they wanted to discard anyway. When you have no answer to the Survival, Stifle what it finds instead — the Orangutan's or the Vigilante's trigger: they get the body, and the threat to the Dreadnought is gone until they find another one, and most lists run one or two.

- Their mana. A turn-one Stifle on a Delta or a Strand against a fifteen- to nineteen-land Gush deck is a Stone Rain, and it is the most tempting play in the deck. Save it for the times it is an advantage you can name. You are fighting on the stack and the fetch is what pays for the next spell, the one you can't answer: Stifle it. They have missed land drops and the land they finally found is a fetch: Stifle it. You kept a hand with three Stifles and one Dreadnought: Stifle it. Otherwise the Stifle has better uses coming, and a turn-one Stone Rain against a deck that makes its land drops is one enabler fewer for the turn that matters.

- Their lock. Forsaken City's upkeep untap, so [Stasis]{Stasis} can't be paid for — against a player who isn't playing around it; see the Stasis matchup for the sequence that does. Gaea's Blessing's shuffle, the most common answer to a Freeze. Their own Brain Freeze's storm trigger: the original resolves, the copies never exist.

- Madness. Stifle the trigger and the card stays exiled: an Arrogant Wurm or a Rootwalla that is never cast, for one mana.

- Creatures that are nothing without their ability. Lackey's damage trigger. Vigilante's flip, Orangutan's entry trigger, and Deranged Hermit's — four Squirrels that never arrive, and a 1/1 they still have to pay echo on. [Siege-Gang]{Siege-Gang Commander}'s tokens. Mother of Runes, the Shapeshifter's discard activation, a Psychatog pump, a Tinkerer activation aimed at the Dreadnought.

- The rest. the soldiers from [Decree]{Decree of Justice}'s cycling trigger, when they have the mana for three or more, Eternal Dragon's return, Specter's discard, Yavimaya Elder's death trigger, an [Oath]{Oath of Druids} trigger, a Portent's delayed draw.

**What it can't touch.** Mana abilities never use the stack, so Lotus Petal, Priest of Titania, Rofellos, Gaea's Cradle and Elvish Spirit Guide are not targets, and neither is a Mox. Static abilities aren't abilities on the stack either: Sphere of Resistance, Humility and Chill can't be Stifled. Replacement effects aren't either — Sutured Ghoul's "as it enters, exile creature cards" never uses the stack, so the Ghoul arrives at full size whatever you hold. And nor can a cost already paid — the Seal's sacrifice is paid before you respond, which is exactly why Stifling it costs them the Seal. Fetchlands are targets because their ability isn't a mana ability.

{cards: Seal of Cleansing | Aura of Silence | Powder Keg | Survival of the Fittest}

{cards: Polluted Delta | Flooded Strand | Hermit Druid | Recurring Nightmare}

{cards: Forsaken City | Deranged Hermit | Basking Rootwalla | Arrogant Wurm}

### Vision Charm (4)

{card: Vision Charm}
The enabler that also protects: in response to the trigger, or in response to removal, or in response to a [Welder]{Goblin Welder} exchange or an [Oath]{Oath of Druids} trigger. It phases out their Dreadnought or Mox too, fogs a turn on defense, and mills four with Brain Freeze. It is good in every Freeze plan for the mill mode, but not always necessary: against decks that go through their own library fast — Enchantress — the four cards it mills matter less than the slot, and against Gaea's Blessing every mill can flip the shuffle, so two can go and Stifle matters more.

**Mode one: an artifact phases out.** The rules first. A phased-out permanent is treated as though it doesn't exist: it can't be targeted, doesn't count as a creature for Oath or Edict, and doesn't take Wrath. It phases in during its controller's untap step, and because phasing doesn't change control it comes back ready to attack. Under [Stasis]{Stasis} there is no untap step, so it never comes back — your own Dreadnought included, so don't Charm it under Stasis unless you mean to lose it. Auras and counters on it phase out with it.

- Your Dreadnought, in response to its trigger or to removal — Swords, a Wrath, a Deed, an Edict, a Welder exchange. Phasing out dodges all of them.

- Your Powder Keg, or a Tsabo's Web in the lists that run it, in response to Disenchant, Naturalize or Overload. The removal fizzles, the Keg phases back in at your untap with its counters, and they are down the card.

- Their Dreadnought, in the mirror, to win the race or to fog a turn.

- Under Stasis, their Black Vise. There is no untap step, so it never phases back in.

- Prison pieces at their end step: Ensnaring Bridge, Sphere of Resistance, Tangle Wire, Winter Orb. Defense Grid is the one prison piece Charm answers badly: the Grid only taxes you on their turn, and a Grid phased out at their end step is back at their untap, so it was never in your way; on their turn the Charm itself costs four. Annul it on cast, Keg it at 2, or Chain it. A Mox, to turn off a color for a turn.

- An animated Mishra's Factory, for tempo.

{cards: Ensnaring Bridge | Tangle Wire | Sphere of Resistance | Winter Orb}

**Mode two: a player mills four.**

- The mill plan. Three Charms mill twelve. A first Brain Freeze as the fourth spell mills twelve more, a second as the fifth spell mills fifteen. Thirty-nine cards for seven mana, no Gush required.

- Mill yourself. In a lost position with five mana: Charm your own library hoping to hit a Flash of Insight, flash it back, find the Counterspell. A long shot. The deck's job in a lost position is to find the out.

- Against Gaea's Blessing decks every mill is a coin flip; hold Stifle for the trigger.
- Against decks that care about the order of their graveyard. Volrath's Shapeshifter has the full text of the top card of its controller's graveyard as long as that card is a creature card, and the Hermit decks line it up so that it enters as a Palinchron or a Karmic Guide and gets the enters-the-battlefield trigger. With the Shapeshifter on the stack, mill them four: if none of the four is a creature, the Shapeshifter enters as itself, and if any is, they choose which goes on top and it enters as that — unless the mill hands them another copy of what they set up, the trigger never happens. And the Hermit decks run Krosan Reclamation to shuffle cards back into an emptied library; after the Reclamation resolves, mill them again.

- After they Portent themselves. They have just arranged their next three draws; four cards off the top sends all of them to the graveyard and gives them three random ones instead. This is the same class of play as Stifling a fetchland: it costs you an enabler or a protection spell, so it is wrong most of the time, and right when you are ahead on cards, board or mana and the job is to stop them catching up. Keep it in mind for exactly that spot.

{cards: Gaea's Blessing | Flash of Insight | Volrath's Shapeshifter | Krosan Reclamation}

**Mode three: lands of one type become a basic type of your choice until end of turn.** The mode with the most text and the least use, and the one that wins the strangest games. It changes every land of the type you name on the table, theirs and yours, and nothing else. In Premodern the only lands with a basic land type are the basics themselves — there are no dual lands, shocklands or anything else that carries "Island" or "Mountain" in its type line — so painlands, fetchlands, Factories, Ports, Forsaken City and most other nonbasics are untouched. The exceptions are the Lairs and the Urza's lands: the type you name doesn't have to be basic, and Lair and Urza's are land types. Against a deck on painlands and fetches, the mode does nothing to their mana.

- Against Goblins with a Siege-Gang Commander, turn their Mountains into Islands at the beginning of combat, before they attack. They can float the red mana in response, but it empties before attackers are declared, so every shot they fire sacrifices a Goblin that then won't attack (and won't pump a Piledriver); they can't have both the combat damage and the shots. Wait until the attack and the floated mana can still go into shots before blocks.

- Against Mono-Blue Frantic Storm, in response to the untapper — Snap, Cloud of Faeries, Frantic Search — turn their Islands into Mountains. The lands untap into the wrong color, nothing blue can be cast off them, and the turn dies.

- A turn's delay. When Peek shows a sorcery-speed threat that needs a color from their basics, turn those basics into Islands on their turn and the spell waits a turn.

- Lairs. Name Lair, and every Lair on the table becomes an Island until end of turn. When a [Rith's Grove]{Rith's Grove} is their only source of a color, it taps for blue instead, and the spell that needed it waits a turn.

- Tron. UW Tron is rare, but you can meet it. Name Urza's and Mountain in their upkeep, and the Mine, the Power Plant and the Tower become Mountains: three red mana instead of seven colorless, for the turn. Mana they float in response empties at the end of the upkeep.

- Splashes with no lands. Simone Fierro won the European Championship with Tranquil Domain in the sideboard and no green source, Charming Islands into Forests to cast it.

{cards: Siege-Gang Commander | Frantic Search | Meditate | Tranquil Domain}

{cards: Rith's Grove | Urza's Mine | Urza's Power Plant | Urza's Tower}

### Counterspell (4)

{card: Counterspell}
The deck's hard counter, and the card you trade one for one with most often. It has two jobs, and every turn you hold it you are choosing between them. The first is protecting the combo: the Counterspell for their answer to the Dreadnought, or for the counter they point at your enabler. The second is tempo — countering their development so that the game is still winnable when you combo. The second job is the one people underrate, because sticking a Dreadnought is not the end of the game: you still have to deal twenty before you are dead, and a board you let resolve can race a 12/12 or gang-block it to death. So there are turns when the right Counterspell target is a 3/3, not because the 3/3 beats you but because the clock it adds does, and reading those turns is most of what playing the card well means. It almost never leaves the deck.

**How to play it.** Counter the card that beats you, not the card in front of you: an engine over a threat, a removal spell aimed at the Dreadnought over a creature, and never a cantrip unless the storm count is the point. In a counter war, count before you start — their free counters against yours, their mana against yours — and remember that the Dreadnought trigger is already on the stack, so you only need the last word. In the mirror, countering the Dreadnought is the default and the conservative choice: it is one of four, while an enabler is one of eight. When you are heavy on countermagic there can be value in fighting over the enabler instead and stripping enablers out of their hand — but every spell in that fight lengthens the storm chain, and a long chain is what their Brain Freeze is waiting for. Weigh it each time. And when the Counterspell mana is up and nothing worth countering comes, that mana becomes an end-of-turn Opt or Impulse.

### Foil (4)

{card: Foil}
Foil's cost is cards, and it is at its best in the plans where cards are not the resource that matters. There are two. Against decks with many answers to a Dreadnought, the plan is often to overload those answers on the commit turn: tap out for Dreadnought and enabler and still counter their Swords and their Disenchant for nothing. You go down two cards for each Foil and it doesn't matter if the Dreadnought connects. And on Freeze plans, the best Freeze turns end a long counter war; every Foil in that war is a spell cast for zero mana that adds to the storm count, and the war needs Foil beside Counterspell to be winnable at all. Pair it with Gush on the commit turn: the two returned Islands are the pitch and the rest of the hand stays as interaction. Foil is trimmed where the pitch cost spends the resource they are attacking — Islands against land destruction and [Sphere]{Sphere of Resistance}, cards against heavy discard — and where the game is attrition with no commit turn to overload.

**How to play it.** Count the interaction left after the pitch. Draw to eight, Dreadnought and Stifle, pitch-cast Foil: three cards remain. Do the same with a Gush in the middle: six remain. The Gush pays for the Foil twice, once in Islands and once in cards. The pitch needs an Island in hand, and the deck has three cards that put one there — Daze, Thwart and Gush. When you hold none of them, it is often right to keep an Island in hand rather than play it, so that Foil stays online: a land drop you don't need is worth less than a free counter you might.

**When to pitch it.** A pitched Foil is three cards for their one: the Foil, the Island and the other card. So pick the spot. Spend it to protect a Dreadnought that is going to win you the game, or to stop a threat that would win them the game. Against anything you can answer another way — a Counterspell or an early Daze on the stack, a Keg once it lands, or Chain of Vapor and a counter when they recast it, two cards for their one — pitching it is often wrong, unless letting it resolve loses you the game on the spot. The main deck has Keg and Chain for the permanents that get through, and the sideboard has many more answers. Sylvan Library is the common example. Its selection is what makes it dangerous, not the extra cards: against you they can rarely pay for more than one a turn, because the life puts them in range of a single Dreadnought hit. Counter it one for one, with a Counterspell, an early Daze or an Annul, but don't spend three cards on it.

And as the game goes on, Foil stops being only a pitch spell. At four or five lands it is a Counterspell you can hard-cast for 2UU, and from then on the question each turn is which of the two costs is cheaper for you right now. The same arithmetic applies to Thwart, whose four mana is the easier cost once you have it.

Two rules on the alternative costs: Sphere of Resistance adds one to them, so Daze, Foil, Gush and Thwart all cost a mana under Sphere; and Defense Grid adds three to spells cast on the opponent's turn, alternative costs included, so under Grid the free counters only work on your own turn.

### Daze (2)

{card: Daze}
The free counter that makes a turn-two Dreadnought on the play work: they Annul, you Daze returning the tapped Island, they can't pay. In game one it bridges to Counterspell mana against Mage, [Sphere]{Sphere of Resistance} and Lackey, and it catches a Terrageddon player's early Mox Diamond or Sylvan Library before you have two Islands up. Whether it stays after sideboarding is a read, not a rule; the Daze counts in the matchup rows are defaults. Opponents try to sneak value engines and cheap threats in before your Counterspell mana is up, and on the play they are often forced to play those into Daze; keep copies on the play unless the plan is a very long, grindy game. If Daze obliterated them in game one they will respect it and play around it, and it comes out. If they tapped out and disrespected it all match, it comes back in for the games you're on the play.

**How to play it.** Return the tapped Island, not the untapped one, so the Stifle mana is still there — and if every Island is untapped, tap one for mana first and return that one; the mana stays in your pool until the end of the step, the same trick as Gush. Daze a spell you don't care about just to get an Island for Foil. Daze your own cantrip when the storm count needs the spell more than you need the card. Keep count of how many Dazes are left in the deck and whether they have tapped out; those are outs you are drawing to, not just cards.

One trap: Elvish Spirit Guide. The Hermit decks — Full English Breakfast, Angry Hermit — run it, and a tapped-out opponent with a Guide in hand exiles it to pay for Daze and keeps the Hermit. Against them, tapped out is not tapped out until you've seen the hand.

{cards: Elvish Spirit Guide | Hermit Druid}

### Thwart (1)

{card: Thwart}
A hard counter that costs three Islands or four mana, and a free counter that feeds Foil: the three Islands it returns are three pitches. That is its best mode — overloading a turn with interaction, Thwart and then Foil and then Foil again off the same lands, more counters than they have answers. Beyond that it is at its best against the decks that attack your lands, where it counters the Armageddon outright or, in response to a Thermokarst, takes the targeted Island home; and against [Stasis]{Stasis}, where three tapped Islands returned to hand are an untapped land drop a turn and two Foil pitches; and as a topdeck after discard, since it needs Islands on the table rather than cards in hand. It is at its worst in tempo games, where giving back three land drops loses, and where Gush is the engine and the two cards compete for the same Islands. In the mirror it stops only one storm copy of a Freeze, where Stifle stops them all, but hard-cast it still counters the Dreadnought or the card-advantage spell.

**How to play it.** Tap the Islands for mana before you return them; the mana stays until the end of the step. In response to an Armageddon you can't counter, three Islands go home and survive it, to be replayed one a turn. Against a Port deck, returning Islands you were going to lose a turn of anyway is nearly free.

{cards: Armageddon | Thermokarst | Stasis | Choke}
