## What this deck is, and where it stands

Mono-Blue Dreadnought, also called Stiflenought after its two key cards, is the most-registered archetype in Premodern since the Parallax Tide ban. In my database of every tournament list from January to October 2026, it is ahead of Sligh, Goblins and Enchantress. It wins with a one-mana 12/12 trampler whose drawback is answered by a one-mana instant, protects that with the cheapest counter suite in the format, and does it on seventeen basic Islands: mana that is consistent, works with every blue card in the deck, and is hard for the opponent's mana denial to attack.

Where it is played matters more than how much. As of October 2026, it is the most played and best-performing deck online: roughly one list in seven, with a match win rate near 60% across thousands of recorded matches, and at the 138-player MTGO Championship Week Finals it won and put four lists in the top 12.

In paper it is a good deck rather than the best one: about one list in twenty, fifth most played, with a match win rate under 50% and no top-16 finish from thirty-one lists at the 326-player Lobstercon. That one surprised me as much as anyone: many players went in with a plan not to lose to the Dreadnought, and it worked. It is the deck to beat, in a format balanced enough that beating it is a plan people can carry out. What it does in paper is convert at big events — it makes the top 8 at about one and a half times its share of the field at tournaments of 100 or more, the best conversion of any deck — with wins at the Portuguese Nationals (on the UW build), Bogardan War II and the European Championship, and top 8s at the Danish and UK Nationals, the New England Regional and the Italian Nationals. Since the ban it has won well over a hundred events of all sizes.

So: the deck to beat online, a top-five deck in paper, and the deck most likely to turn a big-event entry into a top 8.

**Nine words the rest of the primer leans on.**

- *Enabler*: Stifle or Vision Charm, the card that makes the trigger do nothing.
- *Jam*: cast the Dreadnought as early as the hand allows and race.
- *Build-up*: trade your interaction for their key cards until your hand has outgrown theirs, then commit once with everything.
- *Commit turn*: the turn you cast the Dreadnought with the intent of keeping it.
- *Plan B* or *the Freeze plan*: cut the Dreadnoughts and win by milling with Brain Freeze.
- *Storm*: the number of spells cast before Brain Freeze in the same turn, by either player; each one is a copy that mills three more.
- *The pitch*: the Island and the card Foil discards instead of paying its mana.
- *Xerox*: cheap cantrips standing in for lands, which is why the deck runs seventeen.

The argument for the deck is mana efficiency on interaction turns. Dreadnought costs one. Stifle and Vision Charm cost one. Daze, Foil, Gush and Thwart cost nothing. The opponent's answers — Swords, a Blast, a counter, a sweeper — cost as much or more, and they have to find that mana on turns when they also want to develop. Played well, the window in which they can interact gets compressed until they can't.

What it does well:

- A combo that is fast and redundant: four Dreadnoughts, eight enablers, and a two-attack clock.

- Mana that is consistent and works with the spells. Seventeen Islands cast everything on curve, and they are part of the free spells: Daze returns one, Foil pitches one, Gush and Thwart return two and three. Wasteland and the other answers to nonbasics have nothing to hit; what still hurts is the land destruction that hits any land — Stone Rain, Armageddon, Choke — and Rishadan Port, which taps an Island like any other land.

- A counter-dense shell — Counterspell plus seven free counters in Foil, Daze and Thwart — with eleven cantrips, Gush and Flash of Insight to sculpt hands.

- Three real game plans in one 75: jam, control into a protected Dreadnought, or mill with Brain Freeze. The sideboard reshapes the deck rather than patching it.

What beats it: cheap threats that pressure you before your hand comes together, boards that assemble quickly and take over the game, decks that bring many answers to the Dreadnought, and lock pieces that tax or stop your spells. Against a deck that can show ten or more answers to a 12/12 after sideboarding, the Dreadnought plan is a losing one, which is why Brain Freeze is in the 75.
